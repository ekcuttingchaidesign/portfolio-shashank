A gap between two modes

Hitman Cricket had the Blast, five overs of arcade hitting, and the Test modes, long and slow. There was nothing in between.

Players wanted a short match that still feels like a real T20 night: bowlers changing, a field that moves, and a reason to think before swinging.

Three goals

  • Feel like a real match under lights
  • Make the field part of the game
  • Give players something to chase

Limits I designed for

  • A phone held upright
  • One thumb
  • Plays in a browser
  • 60 fps on an iPhone
  • No new controls to learn

How I worked with Claude Code

I worked as designer and director. Claude Code was my build partner: it wrote the game code, and it drafted boards into my Figma file through the Figma connection. Every day ran the same loop, often several times an hour.

The field is the game

In T20 the field decides where the runs are. On a phone you stand behind the batter and can’t see most of the fielders. So the first job was a field that is real, and a way to read it at a glance.

Step one: study real fields

I photographed the field screens of a console cricket game. 19 photos became 18 field cards, turned so you are always in the middle and the bowler is up the screen.

Step two: give every bowler a plan

Next came a T20 field book of 14 fields, each with the situation it is for. Each bowler gets two: his first field, and another for when you keep hitting the same gap.

  • Swing · New-ball attack / Square riders
  • Spinner · Leg-spin strike / Spinner attacking
  • Sling · Short-ball field / Cutters
  • Death · Wide yorker / Straight field

Step three: show it without covering the pitch

Not chosen

A · Always there

A see-through map over the foot of the pitch. Always visible, but it sat on the ground the player watches.

Not chosen

B · Hidden until asked

A pill that opens a sheet. Clean, but nobody opens a sheet in the middle of an over.

Chosen · my idea

C · Two corners

A small live map bottom right, Power Surge bottom left. One tap grows the map out of its corner. The pitch stays as clear as the Blast’s.

  • Fielders red, you blue, whatever the kits
  • A field is set once an over
  • Men stand still on their marks
  • The bowler signals a field change

Your swipe steers the ball

A field only matters if you can aim at the gaps. In Super10 the same swipe that plays a stroke also steers it. Where your thumb lands inside the stroke’s zone is where the ball goes along its arc. Aim too wide and you stretch for it: half the time it comes off the toe of the bat, and the call says so.

  • Same swipes as every mode
  • Keys play the middle of the arc
  • STRETCHED · OFF THE TOE

Ten overs. Full charge.

The logo started as a pink and cyan drawing. I traced it to a vector in Illustrator, then tried it in seven colourways on the game’s black and on cream.

I picked Volt: lime and violet on deep purple. Test Green and Inferno looked good too, but they clashed with the Test modes and with the grass. Then a brand sheet, so every later screen had rules to follow.

The brand sheet

Five ground rules

  • Light, not paint. Grass, pitch and kits stay true
  • Keep the eyeline dark
  • Lime is a budget. A tenth of any view at most
  • No strobe. Three flashes a second at most
  • Wicket means out

This page keeps the same rules.

A night ground, built twice

The first night ground was branded all over. Every addition made it worse, and I took each one down.

  • A violet LED halo along the roof
  • A violet sky
  • The crowd in two sides’ yellow and blue
  • A dark sightscreen
  • Six searchlights over the far end
  • A white ball
  • Greener grass with a darker strip

The plain night stadium looked better than all of it. So I gave Claude Code a photo of a modern ground and we built a new one, the canopy: roofs lit from inside, VIP boxes, a big screen, LED floodlights and a city behind it.

A flat roof read as two planks from the crease. A shell stood on end is the one shape a phone camera sees face on.

LED stumps

Dark plate-violet timber so the ball stands out, SUPER10 reading up each stump, and the light only in the crown, two rings and the bails. They go raspberry the moment a bail lifts.

The death bowler: three builds of one action

Super10 needed a bowler for the last overs, famous for his action. I sent slow-motion clips from behind and side on. It took three builds.

Build 1

The arm dropped back and whipped over. Half a turn.

Build 2

The front arm held out level, then turned behind him like a windmill. Not his.

Build 3 · kept

A short choppy run, the ball arm looping wide and up before a low hop, both arms up, then one full straight turn, quickening to the release.

His over is planned like a real death over: two yorkers, a dipping slower ball, often a slower bouncer, the rest full and quick.

Motion that charges, then rests

The PLAY card

Before launch the card said COMING SOON. I tried two pictures and chose the charged bat, then designed a four-second loop in Figma, keyframe by keyframe.

The bat charges with lime arcs, one arc strikes the PLAY key, the key flashes and a ring pulses out, sparks float off, then it rests. Every four seconds, only while the screen is open.

  • Arcs redrawn every second frame
  • Never more than three flashes a second
  • Still under reduced motion
  • Slams in with a ring of arcs the first time you see it

The announcement film

The first storyboard was flat vector shapes, to be built as a Lottie. I turned it down: it looked like an explainer, not a night match. The film that shipped was made in Remotion with the real game. The kits were filmed on the Dressing Room’s turntable, and the stadium shot comes from the game’s own camera.

  • Lightning strikes on frame one
  • The mark is built inside the first second
  • 10 OVERS · NEW STADIUM · POWER SURGE
  • Team kits sweep on
  • Held on the lockup

After my first viewing, the 5 BOWLERS beat became NEW STADIUM and the intro got faster.

Tier cards

Super10 has its own career ladder, and each rung has its own card material: matte ink, anodised violet, black chrome with lightning, and gold foil. My first Hitman card was Volt foil. Gold won, because gold reads as the top in every mode.

Four things I’ll keep

Show, don’t describe

Slow-motion clips, photos and concept art got the bowler, the field and the kits right far faster than any written brief.

Decide on the real phone

Colours, motion and the field map all changed after I played them on my iPhone. Figma got me close; the phone decided.

Write the rules down

The brand sheet’s five ground rules kept every new screen on brand, even ones I never drew.

Cut the loud version

Searchlights, halos, white pills, a violet sky. The quiet version won every time.
