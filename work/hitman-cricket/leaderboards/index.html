Five overs, then nothing

The first build, 7 to 12 September, was a good thirty balls: swipe, time it, hit sixes. Then the end card showed your score and forgot it. Nothing told you whether 92 was good, and nothing gave you a reason to play the next one.

Before: a number with no neighbours

  • Score shown once, then lost on Play Again
  • No sense of good: is 92 a lot?
  • No identity: every player anonymous
  • Nothing to send a friend but a screenshot

The bet: give every innings a place

  • A public Top 50, one row per player
  • Show the rows either side of yours
  • A name you claim, then carry everywhere
  • Keep every innings, so a career adds up

Three rules before a single pixel

12 to 17 September. Before drawing the board, I settled what a place on it should mean.

“5th has 106”

A score needs neighbours

A place on its own means little. The rows above and below you are what make 101 feel close.

Best only

One row per player

Only your best innings counts, so a bad game never costs you and a good one always might.

No strike rate

Rank what the mode rewards

Fewer balls in a five-over game means you got out. Wickets kept say it the right way round.

The moment a player cares most is right after the innings, so the board comes to them there.

How ties break

  1. Runs
  2. Sixes
  3. Fours
  4. Fewer wickets
  5. Fewer dots
  6. Got there first

Two rows level on runs carry a small note under the name, level · fewer 6s, and light the figure that split them. Colour never carries it alone. All six keys pack into one number, so the phone knows “do I qualify?” without waiting on the network at the one moment a wait would be felt.

One board could never hold five games

16 September to 29 October. Each new mode asked the same question: what is a good innings here? A hundred in Test Survival is a win, not a high score. So every mode got a board ranked on what that mode is about, and two kinds of ladder sat side by side: your best day, and everything you have ever done.

Separate boards, one name

A chase and a five-over slog can’t share a ranking. A name is a person, so it is claimed once and carried onto every board.

Practice stays off

An innings played with any test switch is marked practice on the card and never reaches a board or a career.

Private windows can play, not post

The game still plays. The board politely declines a player it can’t recognise tomorrow.

A best is a maximum, a career is a sum

A sum needs guards a maximum didn’t: each innings counted once, no two within ten seconds, a daily ceiling.

Then the board got a stage

By October the sheet held five modes, nine ladders and a restore offer, all in rows of tabs. In the UI revamp I rebuilt it as a screen of its own, around the same rules.

  • Two dropdowns, not two rows of tabs. One for the mode, one for the ladder. The dots under them say you can swipe to the next mode.
  • The top three get a stage. Spotlights, a crown on first, and the art steps back as the list scrolls up.
  • Your row never scrolls away. “You are #23 · 4 behind #22” stays pinned above the button, which is the next goal, in words.
  • One button, named for the mode. PLAY THE BLAST, not a generic Play, so the board always leads back into a game.

Nine numbers don’t answer “am I any good?” One word does.

Two calls I’d make again

Per-mode thresholds

One shared ladder would have had Survival players climbing 3.5× slower for the same time at the crease. Each ladder costs the same effort: six or seven innings to the second rung.

Founding tiers, not invented runs

The 16 players already on the board when careers shipped got their tier, top five STAR and the next eleven EMERGING, with a line saying why: Founding place · 3rd on the board. Their figures stayed true. Being early should count, and a card built to be shared should never claim runs nobody scored.

A month of records, cashed in on day one

12 October. The Dressing Room unlocks kits and bats for what you have done. Because the boards had counted every innings since September, nothing had to be earned from zero. Players walked in and found bats already theirs, each waiting with its own UNLOCK key.

Design the record first. The rewards design themselves.

A number needs neighbours

The rows either side of yours did more for motivation than the place itself. “One run behind 5th” is a reason to press Play Again.

Segregate by what a mode is about

Each board ranks the thing its players are actually trying to do. Survival ranks the chase, Marathon ranks the team and the batter.

Measure before you set the ladder

The tier thresholds came from five days of real play, not round numbers. Then I picked the top one on purpose, to decide how far away it should feel.

Save the motion for the moments

The board stays calm and legible. The motion goes to the moments it creates: Level Up, the rank hero counting up, the New #1 takeover.
